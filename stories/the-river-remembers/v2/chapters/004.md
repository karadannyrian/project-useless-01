# Chapter 4

The road began to tighten an hour after they broke camp.

The hills drew closer, the scrub thickening into low twisted trees that grew in the angles of the rock. The sea was a constant murmur below, invisible now beyond the cliff's edge. The cart's wheels crunched on coral gravel, the sound carrying in the narrow space.

Terren had fallen silent. His hand rested on his blade.

Alexandria let her gaze move across the terrain the way Hark did—hills, road, sea, hills again. The cliffs were too high for an easy descent. The road was too narrow for the cart to turn. If bandits were going to hit them, they would hit them here.

She loosened her blade in its sheath.

Ahead, Hark had not changed position. He still rode point, his grey horse patient and steady. But his posture had shifted—imperceptibly, the way a blade shifts in a loose sheath. Ready.

Kell called back from his scout position ahead, his voice carrying with unnatural clarity. "Nothing on the road. Can't hear anything in the rocks, but the wind's against me."

Terren grunted. "Keep listening."

The caravan pressed on. The road narrowed further. The gravel was loose, skittering under the horses' hooves, tumbling over the cliff edge into the sea below. The factor's horse balked at a tight turn; Terren leaned over to steady it.

Alexandria's gaze swept the high ground to the right. The hills were a jumble of boulders and low scrub. A hundred places for a man to hide. A hundred places she would have chosen, if she were setting an ambush.

She was reaching for her blade when the first arrow struck the cart.

---

The arrow hit the cart's side with a thud that was louder than it should have been—the sound of iron punching through wood, the vibration carrying through the frame. The factor's horse screamed. A second arrow. A third. One struck the factor's horse in the flank; the animal reared, and the factor tumbled from the saddle, his manifest scattering like white birds into the wind.

Then the shouting started.

They came from the rocks above—six, seven, eight men, maybe more, pouring down the hillside with the momentum of gravity and desperation. Patched leather. Rusted blades. Wild overhand strokes. They were not soldiers. But there were many of them, and the road was narrow, and the cart was already blocking the way forward.

Alexandria's blade was in her hand before she made the decision to draw it. Her body knew the movements. Her mind had stepped back, gone quiet, become a small cold point of observation inside the larger machinery of combat.

She registered details in fragments.

Kell, off his horse, blade out, mouth open in a shout she could not hear over the ringing in her ears.

Terren, dismounted, moving to cover the factor. His Tortoise Shell already forming—the minimal-motion parry pattern that turned the first bandit's wild swing into a stumble and an opened throat.

Hark, ahead, already among them.

She saw him in the spaces between her own engagements.

He was not fighting. Fighting was a contest, an exchange, a thing with rhythm and counter-rhythm. Hark was simply moving, and where he moved, bandits fell.

He did not block. He redirected. His blade arrived at the place where a man's body was about to be, and the man's own momentum carried him onto the steel. The footwork—she knew the footwork, she had been trained in it, she had never seen it used like this. It was not just technique. It was economy elevated to something that looked like stillness, like the world was moving around him and he was simply stepping out of its way.

He killed the first man with a short thrust to the throat. Blade entering and exiting in the same motion. Weight already shifting to the next target.

He killed the second man with a cut across the back of the knee. Downward thrust. Almost gentle in its precision.

He killed the third man with an elbow to the face and a blade that seemed to appear in the man's chest without having traveled the distance between.

His face was utterly blank. Not angry. Not afraid. Not anything.

She could not watch him. She had her own work.

A bandit came at her with a wood-axe, the blade heavy and slow. She stepped inside the swing—Void Step, automatic, the shuffle that kept her center low—and put her blade through his armpit. The axe fell. The man made a sound that was not a word and sat down heavily, his back against the cart.

She was already turning to the next.

Another bandit. Younger. Face a mask of fear and fury. Short sword swung with both hands, overhand, his whole body behind it. She parried. The impact jarred up her arm, into her shoulder. She redirected—White Water principle, learned but never used in live combat—and his momentum carried him past her. She put her blade into his back. He fell without a sound.

Her breathing was steady. Her hands were steady. Her mind was that small cold point, cataloging.

Then the second wave came.

They had been hidden further up the hillside, waiting for the escorts to commit. Four more men. Fresher. Better armed. They hit the rear of the caravan, where Alexandria stood alone. Terren was still at the cart, guarding the factor. Kell was somewhere ahead, fighting. Hark was ahead, cutting through the first wave.

She was alone.

She killed the first one—a big man with a spear—by taking the spearpoint on her blade and sliding inside his reach, her knife finding his throat.

But the second and third came together. She could not engage both. Her Void Step took her backward, toward the cliff edge. Her boot skidded on loose gravel. The sea was very loud below her. The fourth man was coming from the side.

She could not block all three.

Her mind registered something that was not a threat assessment.

*This is the moment. This is the one that matters.*

She raised her blade. She would take the closest one with her. That was the calculation. The cold point made it without emotion.

Then the man on the left stopped.

A blade was sticking out of his chest. He looked down at it with an expression of mild surprise, and then he fell forward, and Hark was standing behind him.

He killed the second man with a cut across the throat—the same short, precise movement, the blade arriving at the place where the man was about to be. The third man turned to face him and Hark did something with his footwork—a shuffle, a shift of weight—that put him inside the man's guard before the man could finish his swing. The blade went in under the ribs. The man folded.

The fourth man ran. Hark let him go.

The road was silent. The ringing in her ears was fading. The sea was still loud below.

Hark looked at her. His face was still blank, but there was something in his eyes—a flicker, a question.

"No," she said.

He nodded once. Then he turned and walked back toward the front of the caravan, stepping over the bodies without looking at them.

---

She walked among them.

Not to loot. To see them. To fix them in her memory the way she fixed everything. The dead were data. Data could be processed.

The man she had killed first, the one with the axe. He was older than she had thought, grey in his beard, his hands callused from work that was not violence. A farmer, maybe, or a fisherman. His eyes were open.

She closed them without knowing why she did it.

The young one with the short sword. He was younger than her. His face was still a mask of fear. She had put her blade through his back. He had died running toward her, or past her, or—she did not know. She would never know.

The big man with the spear. The one Hark had killed from behind. His blood was mixing with the coral gravel, turning it dark. The smell of it was in her throat—copper and salt and the faint ammonia of voided bladders.

She cataloged the dead and moved on.

Terren was kneeling beside the factor's horse. The animal was screaming—a high, terrible sound that cut through the silence. The factor stood a few paces away, his manifest clutched against his chest, the pages bent and blood-spattered. His mouth was moving. No sound came out.

Kell was sitting on a rock at the side of the road, his blade across his knees, his head tilted as if listening to something very far away. Blood on his sleeve—not his own. His face was older than it had been an hour ago.

Vallus was standing near the cart.

He had not fought. His blade was still sheathed. His hands were clenched at his sides, the knuckles white. He was staring at the bodies with an expression Alexandria recognized: the blank horror of someone who had never seen violent death and was discovering, in real time, that it was nothing like the stories.

He looked at her as she approached. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"I—" he said. "I couldn't—"

She did not know what to say to him. Comfort was not a skill she possessed. She said: "The factor needs assistance. His horse is wounded."

Vallus blinked. The instruction seemed to steady him—a task, something to do. He nodded jerkily and moved toward the factor.

She logged: *Vallus Kellan: combat-ineffective. Froze under fire. Not unexpected. Civilian.*

She dismissed the other thing—the thing she had felt when she saw him standing there, unharmed, while the bodies cooled around him. It was not contempt. It was not disappointment. It was something she did not have a word for.

Hark had not moved from his position at the front of the caravan. His blade was sheathed. His gloves were still on. He stood with his back to the rock face, his gaze on the road ahead, as if the ambush were not over, as if there might be more coming. The bodies around him were not his concern.

She did not approach him. She did not know what she would say if she did.

He had saved her life. That was a fact. It was also a debt. She did not know how to carry a debt she had not chosen to incur.

She walked back toward the cart. The factor's horse had stopped screaming. Terren had cut its throat.

---

No one spoke.

The caravan had limped another two hours along the coast before Terren called the halt. The factor rode double with Terren. Vallus rode in silence, his eyes on the road, his hands still clenched. Kell had said nothing since the ambush, his usual chatter replaced by a watchful quiet. Hark had not spoken at all.

Alexandria sat with her back to a boulder, her blade across her knees. She had cleaned it three times. The blood was gone. She was still cleaning it.

Her hands were trembling.

She noticed the tremor the way she noticed everything—as data. Her fingers were shaking, very slightly, a fine vibration that had not been there before. She tried to still them. They would not still.

She noted: *Tremor in both hands. Onset post-combat, duration unknown. Probable cause: adrenaline aftereffect. Not injury-related. Will resolve with rest.*

She cleaned the blade a fourth time.

Kell dropped down beside her. He was not stretching. He was not grinning. He sat with his back to the same boulder, his shoulder a few inches from hers, and said nothing.

After a while, he said: "I heard you. At the end. When they flanked you. Your heartbeat went very fast and then very slow. That's the bad moment. When it goes slow. That's when the body knows."

She did not answer.

"I've never been in a fight like that. I've trained. I've drilled. I've never—" He stopped. "I killed one man. One. Hark killed five. You killed three. Terren killed two. I killed one, and I heard everything. Every single sound. The blade going in. The sound he made. The—" He stopped again. His hands were steady on his knees. "I can still hear it. I'm going to keep hearing it."

She said: "You held your position. You're alive. That's the metric."

"That's a cold metric."

"Yes."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he almost smiled—the ghost of his usual grin, there and gone. "You're not good at this. The talking thing."

"No."

"Me neither, right now. So we'll just sit."

They sat. The sea sounded below. The stars were beginning to emerge, faint through the grey. The tremor in her hands was still there. She did not try to still it anymore.

Hark was sitting across the hollow from her, his back to the stone, his blade sheathed. He was not sharpening it. He was not moving. He was looking at nothing, or at something only he could see. His gloves were still on.

She thought about the way he had moved during the fight. The footwork that was not footwork but survival, pared down to its absolute minimum. The blankness of his face. The way he had stepped over the bodies without looking at them.

She thought about the way he had looked at her, after, and asked the question without asking it. *Are you hurt?* The flicker in his eyes. The question she had not expected.

She did not know what to do with any of this. She filed it all under his name and did not examine the file.

The tremor in her hands was still there. She did not try to still it.
