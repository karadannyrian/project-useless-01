# CHAPTER 13: THE SERPENT

---

The contract was written on thick, salt-damp parchment that smelled of fish tallow and old ink. Graen pushed it across the scarred oak of the Factorage table with his stubby fingers, his gold rings clicking against the wood.

"The fishing collective pooled their coin for this one," Graen said, his voice a low rumble above the scratch of Verrol’s ledger quill in the corner. "Three cutters lost in as many weeks. The outer island routes are completely choked. The boys are terrified to drop their nets."

Hark looked down at the ink. A reef serpent. Forty feet of territorial muscle, according to the scribbled testimonies of the survivors. It wasn't his usual trade. He preferred caravan work—dry land, predictable paths, and targets that bled when they were supposed to. High-risk maritime hunts were a young man’s game or a desperate man's coin.

"Verrol’s already drawing the names," Graen continued, squinting up through the haze of pipe smoke. "He wants steady hands on the lines. I didn't think it was your speed, Hark."

"I'll take it," Hark said.

Graen’s hand froze on his pipe. His thick eyebrows rose. "You will? Usually takes three pints of dark ale and a bonus voucher to get you near a boat."

"The pay is sufficient," Hark said, his voice flat. He didn't offer anything else. He didn't look at Verrol, whose quill had stopped mid-stroke.

His reasons were a tangled knot he chose not to examine. The coin was necessary—it always was—but the restlessness in his shins was new. For three weeks, he had sat across from Alexandria in the yard, watching her move, watching her breathe, carrying the crushing weight of what he had deduced in the scribe's shop. She was a Silencer. A hound of the Order, sent to map the shadows of Salthaven. And he was training her.

She required field experience. Real blood, real iron, against something that didn't have a human throat to bargain with. He told himself this was practical. If she died because she lacked the stomach for a monster, his assignment with House Kellan was a failure.

He told Vallus the same lie an hour later in the library. The young nobleman had looked up from his ledger, his soft hands hovering over the clean paper, his face tightening with standard Kellan hesitation.

"The outer islands?" Vallus asked, his brow furrowing. "The rumors say the thing is massive, Hark. She's only a junior escort. Her duties are here, within the estate walls."

"The estate walls don't bite," Hark said, leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed over his chest, his leather gloves tucked securely into his belt. "She has the form down. Her balance is steady. But she hasn't smelled a predator yet. If you want her to protect your family's investments, she needs to see what happens when the iron hits the skin."

Vallus looked at the floor, then back at Hark. He sighed, a small, trusting sound. "If you say she's ready, Hark. I trust your judgment."

He shouldn't have. Hark turned and left before the nobleman could change his mind.

---

The harbor at dawn was a grey soup of fog and rotting kelp. The *Sea Warden* lay low in the water, her timber frame creaking against the stone pier as the tide rolled in from the shelf.

Hark stood by the gangplank, watching the team arrive. Jera was already on board, sitting on an overturned bait crate. She was a Red Coin duelist in her thirties, her dark hair sheared short to keep it out of her eyes. She didn't look up when Hark approached; she was busy running an oiled cloth down the edge of her side-sword, her movements small, precise, and entirely devoid of wasted energy.

Tomas arrived next. He was an Iron Rib fighter, broad-shouldered and thick-necked, carrying a heavy timber-pended harpoon over one shoulder like a walking stick. He gave Hark a single, heavy nod. His eyes flicked down to Hark's gloved hands, then back to his face. He didn't ask why the mercenary wore heavy hide in the summer damp. They had worked a caravan route near the Salt Flats two years ago; Tomas knew Hark had his rules, and Tomas was a man who respected a closed door.

Captain Rallis stood at the helm, his old hands locked onto the wheel. His eyes were hollow, rimmed with red, staring out into the grey channel where the fog met the sea. Two weeks ago, his eldest boy had gone into the water when the serpent struck their skiff. The boy hadn't come back up. Beside him, two young deckhands—brothers by the look of their shared crooked noses—were sweating through their wool shirts as they hauled three-pronged iron harpoons onto the deck. Their fingers trembled against the wood. They were trying to look like men, but they smelled of raw fear.

Alexandria arrived last.

She walked down the stone pier with her short blade slung low at her left hip, her heavy wool escort coat buttoned tightly against the morning chill. Her face was a mask of cold grey stone. She didn't look at the fog, or the ship, or the blood-tubs on the deck. She met Hark's eyes for a fraction of a second—a sharp, penetrating glance that felt less like a greeting and more like an assessment—before she stepped onto the gangplank.

She had been different since the scribe's shop. More silent. More watchful. Hark watched her take her position near the mainmast, her hand resting naturally on the pommel of her blade.

---

The cutter cut through the grey swell, the salt spray gathering on Hark’s eyelashes as they cleared the harbor mouth. Salthaven shrank into a smear of grey slate and dark stone behind them.

The channel between the outer islands was a treacherous stretch of black limestone teeth and shallow coral atolls. The water here was deceptively clear, shifting from slate grey to a brilliant, terrifying turquoise where the reef shelf rose to meet the keel.

Captain Rallis spat into the foam, pointing a scarred finger toward a jagged ridge of coral just below the surface. "There," the old man rasped. "Look at the white score marks on the rock. That's from the scales. It rubs itself against the limestone to clear the barnacles."

Hark walked to the center deck, his boots finding the wet wood with practiced ease. "Tomas, Jera—man the forward harpoon mounts. Keep the pivot screws loose. If it comes up beneath us, you won't have time to fight a stuck gear."

The two mercenaries moved without a word.

Hark turned to the deckhand brothers. "Start the chum line. Pour the offal over the low side, ten feet apart. Don't stop until I tell you."

The younger brother began tipping the wooden tubs, dumping a dark, thick slurry of fish blood and entrails into the turquoise water. The scent was immediate—heavy, metallic, and foul.

Alexandria stood at Hark's shoulder, her back straight against the roll of the sea. She didn't flinch from the smell. Her weight was balanced over her heels, her feet slightly apart—the exact geometry of the Void Step he had beaten into her legs for three weeks.

"If it takes the boat," Hark said to her, his voice low enough to stay between them, "you don't swim for the deep water. You find the limestone ridge and you climb. Understood?"

"Understood," she said. Her voice was steady. Too steady for a girl looking at a blood-slick sea.

Hark looked away, his jaw tightening. He didn't think about his Gate. He never thought about the fire. For twenty-two years, the fire had been a door he kept barred from the inside. The wood was thick; the iron bolts were rusted shut. He didn't look at the key. He didn't think about the heat that lived under his skin, waiting for a single crack in his resolve.

---

The sea didn't part; it fractured.

A massive surge of slate-grey muscle erupted from the chum line, forty feet of coiled armored hide exploding through the turquoise surface. The head rose higher than the cutter's mainmast, a blunt, prehistoric wedge of bone and blind, pale eyes.

The *Sea Warden* heaved violently to port. The younger deckhand lost his footing on the fish slime, his boots sliding out from beneath him as he hit the low rail and rolled into the white foam.

"Boy!" Rallis screamed, his hands tearing at the wheel as the serpent's belly slammed against the hull.

Tomas didn't hesitate. His wide back twisted as he launched the forward harpoon. The three-pronged iron flew true, burying itself deep into the serpent's flank with a heavy, wet *thunk*. The creature roared—a high, whistling shriek that vibrated through the deck planks—and its tail thrashed, sending a wall of grey water over the bow.

Jera moved like oil. She caught the second harpoon mount, pivoted the iron, and drove it into the creature's lower tail section as it coiled. "Line's caught!" she shouted through the spray.

Hark was already at the rail, his long steel blade drawn. He didn't look for a killing strike; he timed his cuts to the creature's lunges, hacking at the thick tendons near the jaw to force its massive head away from the ship's center line, steering it toward the white sandbanks of the shallows.

Alexandria was a shadow beside him. Her short blade was a blur of Red Coin precision, finding the soft, pale skin between the overlapping slate scales with every thrust. She was good. Better than she had been in the yard. Her movements were clean, cold, and entirely focused on the geometry of the target.

Then the serpent twisted.

The massive tail came about in a blind, horizontal whip-crack. The timber rail disintegrated. The older deckhand went down with a sickening crack as the wood splintered against his thigh, his leg bending where it shouldn't. Jera was lifted off her feet, her body slamming hard against the mainmast before she slid to the deck, dazed and bleeding from the temple.

Tomas groaned, his thick arms straining against the iron line as the serpent pulled the cutter toward the deep channel.

Alexandria was at the broken rail, her blade raised, repositioning her feet for a downward strike into the creature's eye—

The secondary roll of the tail caught her from the flank.

It wasn't a direct hit, but the mass was too great. The force lifted her clear of the deck. Hark saw her boots clear the shattered wood, her grey coat billowing as she hit the white foam three yards out. She didn't scream. She went under hard, the cold grey water closing over her head in a swirl of red chum and white froth.

The serpent's blunt head turned toward the splash.

---

For a fraction of a second, the salt spray was gone.

The smell of fish blood and rotting kelp vanished. The wood beneath Hark’s boots turned to frozen moss and pine needles. The sky wasn't grey fog anymore; it was a vast, terrifying vault of black winter night, filled with sharp, uncaring stars.

He was ten years old. His knees were sinking into the snow.

A girl with grey eyes was lying on the ground in front of him, her wool coat dark with blood where the crossbow bolt had pierced her center chest. Her lips were blue. Her breath was leaving her in a small, final gust of white air—the last, desperate working of a Wind mage who had nothing left but the throat.

He was clawing at her shoulders, his small fingers slick with her warmth. He was opening his Gate. He was screaming for the light to come, for the fire to stay, but the light was a blind thing, and it wasn't enough. It had never been enough.

***"Lea!"***

The name tore out of his ribs like a broken bone. It was a howl, a raw, jagged sound he had not permitted his throat to make in twenty-two standard years. It was the sound of a man watching the dark take the only thing he had ever chosen to protect.

He opened his Gate.

The heat came roaring up from the locked place in his chest, and the cold followed it—a rush of ice through his palms, a sudden, brutal settling of the debt. His gloves frosted over before they burned. The skin beneath went numb. The ledger was open.

He didn't look for Light. Light required geometry, lens-craft, and a still mind. He had none of them. He reached for the base state. Raw, unrefined fire.

He didn't throw it. He aimed the lance of heat directly at the turquoise water surrounding the serpent's snout, opening his palms to the sea.

The water didn't warm; it exploded. A massive, roaring column of white steam erupted from the surface, flash-boiling thirty feet of ocean in a single heartbeat. The hiss was deafening, a localized thunderclap that shook the cutter's remaining timbers.

The serpent, its delicate gill slits and pale eyes caught in the sudden thermal shock, aborted its dive. It shrieked, its massive body thrashing in agony as the scalding water blistered its hide, and it turned away from the sinking grey coat, fleeing toward the deep, cold blue of the outer channel.

Hark didn't wait for the steam to clear. He was already over the broken rail.

He hit the water hard, the freezing sea a violent shock against his skin after the heat of the Gate. He dived down into the churning, milky blindness of the boiling foam. His hands clawed through the current, searching through the dark until his fingers caught heavy wool—a collar, a sodden sleeve.

He locked his hand into her coat and hauled her upward.

---

He broke the surface with his fingers knotted into Alexandria's collar. Tomas was at the broken rail, his massive arms reaching down to seize the girl by the shoulders and hoist her onto the wet deck. Hark scrambled up behind her, his boots dragging over the splintered timber.

Alexandria fell onto her hands and knees, coughing up ropes of grey seawater, her chest hitching as her lungs fought for air. She was alive.

Hark stayed on his knees beside her, his head hanging low between his shoulders, his breath coming in ragged, steaming gasps that smelled of ozone and scorched skin.

His gloves were gone.

The base-state fire had come directly from his palms, and the thick leather hadn't been treated for that kind of ledger. The hide had vaporized from the inside out, leaving nothing but black, charred rings of wool around his elbows where his sleeves had ended.

His hands were completely bare.

The **frost-scars** were fully visible in the grey morning light. They were thick, raised networks of dead, white tissue, running like frozen spiderwebs from his knuckles all the way to his forearms. They were dense, old, and unmistakable—the permanent ledger of a Fire-mage who had spent decades borrowing heat from his own flesh to pay for his workings.

Everyone was looking.

Jera, wiping the blood from her forehead near the mast, froze. Her fingers stayed pinned to her temple, her eyes locked on his bare skin. Tomas, still holding the harpoon line, went perfectly still, his heavy jaw tightening into a hard line.

Captain Rallis looked down from the helm. His old face shifted through three different shapes—fear, confusion, and then something heavier, something complicated that settled between his eyes like an old debt. The two deckhands, one clutching his shattered leg on the deck, could only stare with wide, wet eyes at the man who had just boiled the sea.

The serpent was gone. A long slick of scorched oil and dark blood marked its path as it retreated into the deep channel, driven off by the shock.

Alexandria wiped the salt from her mouth with the back of her sleeve. She looked up.

She saw the scars.

She heard him call her *Lea*.

She did not corrected him. She didn't speak. She didn't pull away. She knew who that ghost was.

Hark looked back at her, his hands braced against the wet wood of the deck. His secret was out. He had used magic in front of seven witnesses, without a mask, without a lie to cover the heat. He waited for her to flinch. He waited for her to rise into the cold, clinical threat-geometry of an Order Silencer preparing a terminal extraction.

She didn't move. She held his gaze for five long breaths, her grey eyes reflecting the grey sky, and then she looked down at her own pale fingers, still dripping with Salthaven water.

Neither of them spoke.

---

Jera broke the silence. She stood up, her side-sword clicking against her thigh, her voice as sharp and pragmatic as her steel.

"Anyone else here planning to report this to the Registry?" she asked, looking around the deck.

Tomas looked at Hark's hands, then turned his back to the rail, staring out at the empty channel. "I didn't see anything," the Iron Rib said, his voice level. "The water was churning. The serpent thrashed. There was a lot of steam from the blood-tubs. A lot of confusion."

Captain Rallis spat over the side. He walked down from the helm, his old boots heavy on the wood, and stopped three feet from Hark.

"My report to the fishing collective will say the beast was driven off by a harpoon and a fire-pot," Rallis said. He looked down at the white webs on Hark's arms. "The fire-pot was my idea. We carry oil in the bow for the lanterns." The old man touched the brim of his salt-stained hat. "My boy is at the bottom of this channel, mercenary. You just pulled that girl out of it. I don't care what you are. The debt is paid."

The younger deckhand nodded mutely from the corner, his teeth chattering against his lip. They owed Hark their lives; they wouldn't talk.

Jera turned her cold eyes toward the center deck. "What about you, junior escort? Your house has a lot of ledgers."

Alexandria didn't answer immediately. She stood up slowly, her wet coat hanging heavy around her shins. She looked at Hark's forearms one last time before he could hide them.

"He saved my life," she said. Her voice was hoarse from the salt, but it didn't shake. "That's all I saw."

The lie hung between them on the damp air—solid, heavy, and forbidden.

Hark didn't thank her. He couldn't. The words were all part of a vocabulary he had buried with Lea. He reached into his pack, pulled out two strips of rough linen cloth, and wrapped them around his bare hands, covering the white scars from view. He walked to the bow without a word to watch the grey swell for signs of another ridge.

---

The *Sea Warden* limped back into Salthaven harbor as the late afternoon light turned the slate roofs to dull copper.

The deckhands were bandaged with old sailcloth. Jera sat by the hatch, a dark blue bruise spreading across her jawline, her fingers steady as she continued to oil her blade. Tomas sat three feet from Hark at the bow, his heavy shoulders resting against the timber rail. He didn't speak. The silence was a gift Hark hadn't expected from a sellsword.

Alexandria sat alone by the mainmast, her back braced against the thick pine, her eyes closed. She wasn't sleeping. Her breathing was too rhythmic, too controlled—the internal pattern of someone holding their reservoir behind a locked door.

Hark watched her from the bow. His hands ached beneath the linen wraps, the old cold in his chest waking up as the heat of the Gate subsided.

His rules were in pieces around his boots. He had used the fire. He had screamed her name. He had dove into a blood-slick sea to save a Silencer, and now she held his life between her fingers like a dry leaf.

He didn't know what was left of a man when his rules were gone.

Alexandria opened her eyes. She looked across the six feet of wet deck that separated them—a distance that felt like twenty years of dark water. She didn't speak. She didn't move.

But she didn't look away.
