## Chapter 1: Arrival

**POV:** Alexandria

---

**Beat 1 — The Approach**

Alexandria stands at the ship's rail as Salthaven resolves from the sea-haze. She does not think *Here I am* or *The mission begins*. She thinks about the harbor's shape—a crescent, defensible only from the landward side, vulnerable to any fleet that cared to close the arc. She notes the limestone spine jutting into the bay, the way the town climbs it in tiers: docks at the waterline, market and trade houses above, a scatter of rooftops rising to a ridge where the road vanishes inland. The ropewalk is visible even from this distance—a long, low shed running parallel to the shore, its roof patched in three places. She catalogs exits by instinct. Narrow streets. Flat roofs with low parapets. A single main road that switches back twice before reaching the ridge. The town is smaller than she expected. Less defended. She corrects herself: *Less observed.* That is the point. That is why she is here.

The ship's crew moves around her. She has been invisible for the entire crossing—a quiet girl with a single pack, too young to be interesting, too plain to be memorable. She has practiced invisibility the way other girls practice instruments. She is very good at it.

---

**Beat 2 — The Dock**

Her boots hit stone. The harbor smells of fish and hemp and salt-crusted wood, the sour undernote of low tide. She threads through dockworkers hauling nets and fishmongers arranging their morning catch on beds of kelp. Her pack is light. Her posture is unremarkable—shoulders relaxed, gait unhurried. She is a junior escort for a minor merchant house, arriving ahead of her employers to prepare for their trade season. She wears that identity like a coat that fits well enough. It is not her first coat. It will not be her last.

She registers faces without interest—the automatic categorization of someone trained to identify threats before they identify her. A one-armed fisherman mending a net. A boy of maybe twelve unloading crates, his movements quick and practiced. A woman in a dark shawl, her back turned, her hands busy with something Alexandria cannot see. No one looks at her twice. Good.

---

**Beat 3 — The Town**

The climb into Salthaven proper takes her through the market square, still sparse at this hour. She notes the stalls that will fill by midday, the positions of the permanent structures, the alleys that cut between them. The Factorage tavern squats at the square's edge, its iron sign—a set of scales with one pan broken off—creaking in the salt breeze. She files the name, the location, the number of windows. Mercenaries take contracts there. She will need to visit, eventually. Not today.

The ropewalk dominates the lower town, its long shed exhaling the smell of tar and hemp. She slows her pace as she passes it—not obviously, not enough to draw attention. The shed's doors are open. Inside, men walk backward down the length of the building, twisting fibers into rigging, their hands moving in rhythms too practiced to be conscious. The smell is stronger here, sharp and resinous. Above the shed, a narrow window, a shutter slightly ajar. Someone lives there. She notes the proximity to the harbor, the number of exits, the lack of street-facing windows on the ground floor. A defensible position. She moves on.

---

**Beat 4 — Vara's Shop**

The scribe's shop is small, wedged between a chandlery and a boarded-up storefront. The sign says *Vara's Scripts* in faded gold lettering. Inside: ink, old paper, the dry smell of a room that does not see much sun. Shelves line the walls, stuffed with ledgers and loose scrolls. A counter separates the public space from the work area, where a woman sits with her back to the door, copying something in a neat, unhurried hand.

Vara is older than Alexandria expected. Grey hair pulled back from a face that has settled into lines of quiet endurance. Her fingers are stained at the tips—ink, not age. She does not smile. She does not rise. She turns, looks Alexandria over with a gaze that is neither warm nor cold, and says: "You're the new one."

"Yes."

The debrief is brief. Vara does not offer tea. She does not ask about the crossing. She recites the mission parameters in a voice that suggests she has done this before, many times, and has learned not to invest in the outcome. Observe. Catalog. Report. Do not engage. Alexandria is a probe, not a weapon—a distinction Vara emphasizes with the faintest curl of her lip, as if she finds the distinction meaningless but is required to make it.

She receives a key to a rented room above a cooper's shop on the east side of town. A list of local contacts—names she will memorize and destroy before morning. A schedule for her first week with House Kellan. And a warning, delivered without emphasis: "Salthaven keeps its secrets. Don't expect them to open for you."

Alexandria nods. She does not ask what secrets Vara means. She has been trained to find secrets. She will find them.

---

**Beat 5 — House Kellan**

The Kellan compound sits on the lower slope of the ridge, a modest cluster of buildings around a central courtyard. It has the look of a house with aspirations rather than history—the walls recently whitewashed, the gate new enough that its hinges do not yet creak. A servant leads her to a receiving room where Vallus Kellan is waiting.

He is young. Early twenties, she estimates, with the soft hands of someone who has never held a sword in earnest and the eager posture of a man trying to fill a role too large for him. He introduces himself with a full name and title, as if she might not know who he is. He asks her name. She gives it—Alexandria Whittier, the name the foundling house gave her, the name the Order never changed because names are useful. He asks about her experience. She recites her cover: trained in basic escort work, familiar with trade routes, looking for steady employment. He seems satisfied, or at least willing to pretend.

He assigns her a room in the compound, a schedule, a place in the house's escort rotation. There is an expedition to the outer islands in three days—supplies, payroll, a mining outpost the house is hoping to bring into its trade network. She will be part of the escort detail. "It's not dangerous," Vallus says, with the confidence of someone who has never been in danger. "Just routine." Alexandria nods. She has read the reports on the route. She knows it is not routine.

She is now a junior escort for House Kellan. The identity settles over her like a second coat. It fits. It does not warm her.

---

**Beat 6 — The Market at Dusk**

She does not go straight to her room. The light is failing, and the market is thinning, but she walks it anyway—reconnaissance disguised as idleness. The stalls are closing. A fisherwoman hauls baskets of silver catch toward the smokehouse. A spice merchant weighs his remaining stock, scooping cinnamon bark into jars. The air tastes of salt and woodsmoke and the faint sweetness of overripe fruit.

She passes a textile stall where a woman is buying children's clothing—small tunics, a woolen blanket. The woman's hands are wrapped in bandages, the linen clean but worn, covering her palms and knuckles. She counts coins carefully, her voice low. Alexandria notes the bandages without breaking stride, files the detail: *healer, probably, hands marked by work.* The woman finishes her purchase, tucks the bundle under her arm, and turns away.

Alexandria watches her disappear into a side street, then moves on. A healer buying children's clothes. Unremarkable. She has a mission to conduct.

---

**Beat 7 — The Factorage at Night**

She stops outside the tavern with the broken-scale sign. The window is smeared with salt and old smoke, but she can see through it well enough. Inside: a low-ceilinged room, tables of scarred wood, a fire burning in a hearth too large for the space. Mercenaries drink in clusters, their weapons propped within reach. A thin clerk writes in a ledger at a corner table, his pen moving in precise, unhurried strokes. A heavier man—the guildmaster, she assumes—sits at a table near the back, eating walnuts from a clay bowl and watching the room with the lazy attention of someone who has been watching the same room for twenty years.

She catalogs the regulars. The Iron Rib fighter in the corner, his shoulders too broad for his chair. The Red Coin duelist near the fire, her blade on the table beside her cup. The Unorthodox scrappers playing dice by the bar, their movements quick and nervous. And one man, alone, near the back wall.

He is not drinking. He is not playing dice. He sits with his back to the wall, facing the door, his hands gloved despite the warmth of the hearth. Long sleeves, despite the season. His stillness is not rest—it is readiness, the kind of stillness she has been trained to recognize because she has been trained to embody it. He is watching the door. She does not know his face. She does not know his name. But something in her chest tightens, a half-second of recognition that she cannot explain and does not try to.

She does not enter. She is not ready to be seen yet. She files him under *interesting* and walks on.

---

**Beat 8 — The Room**

The cooper's shop is dark when she reaches it. She climbs the stairs to the rented room, unlocks the door, and steps inside. The room is small: a cot, a washstand, a window that faces the street. She checks the window first—the latch, the sightlines, the drop to the alley below. Then the door, the lock, the gap at the threshold. The walls are thin. She can hear the cooper moving in the shop below, the scrape of a tool on wood, the creak of floorboards.

She sits on the cot. Removes her boots. The quiet is familiar. It is the same quiet she has lived in her whole life—the quiet of dormitories, of training floors, of the Hollowing where children learned to be empty. She does not have a word for loneliness. She has a word for readiness. She is ready.

But she cannot stop thinking about the man with the gloves. The way he watched the door. The way his stillness mirrored hers. She has seen a hundred mercenaries, a thousand fighters, and none of them have made her pause like that. She does not know why. She does not like not knowing.

She lies down. The cooper's work has stopped. The town is silent. Tomorrow, she will report to the Kellan compound. Tomorrow, she will begin cataloging in earnest. Tomorrow, she will see the gloved man again—at the Factorage, or on the docks, or in the market—and she will observe him, and she will file him, and she will understand what made her pause.

Tonight, she is still a stranger in a town that keeps its secrets.

She does not sleep for a long time.
